TQMI put AI to work inside its consulting delivery

TQMI's consultants produce document-heavy, evidence-bound deliverables for industrial and manufacturing clients. Caizin placed Anthropic's Claude on the three surfaces those consultants already work in and ran the rollout on live client deliverables. Ten use cases are documented so far, and on the tasks consultants measured, 25 to 75 percent of the time came out.

Engagement summary

What was done, and what came of it.

Client
TQMI is a management consulting firm serving industrial and manufacturing organizations in quality, operational excellence, and business performance. Its engagements run from Deming Prize programmes and quality-circle rollouts to sales-process design and shop-floor problem solving.
Engagement
Caizin was engaged to get AI into TQMI's consulting delivery. The test was a narrow one: whether a consultant working on a live client deliverable, against a deadline, could produce better work in less time while keeping control of quality.
What was deployed
Claude, Anthropic's AI assistant, on three surfaces that match how TQMI's consultants already work: a chat window for research and thinking, a workspace for building deliverables from source material, and Claude inside MS Office for documents, decks, and spreadsheets. Caizin also set the working rules for which surface suits which job and for consultant review of anything a client sees.
Early results
Ten use cases across four consultants, covering sales analytics, workforce attrition, operational exception detection, a client process manual, competitor benchmarking, Deming Prize deliverables, quality-circle presentations, post-programme training support, and pre-visit assessment research. On the tasks that were measured, 25 to 75 percent of the time came out, and consultants reported more rigorous and more consistent output.
Background

An adoption programme, run on real client work.

Caizin is a strategy-led, execution-driven partner for growth-stage and mid-market companies, and it stays embedded through delivery rather than handing over a set of recommendations. AI adoption has become part of that delivery work. With TQMI it was the entire engagement.

The question on the table was not general AI capability. It was whether an experienced consultant, working on a deliverable a client was waiting for, could get more done without lowering the standard of what went out. That can only be answered on live engagements, so the programme ran on real client work from the start.

Where the consulting time was going

A single TQMI engagement can generate process manuals, benchmarking studies, root-cause analyses, executive readouts, and multi-year improvement roadmaps. Each one has to be accurate, specific to the client's business, and robust enough to hold up when the client's leadership pushes back on it.

Three costs follow from that. Research and drafting absorb days or weeks before a client has anything to react to. Turning findings into a clear, client-ready format is manual work that tends to land on senior people. And a large programme carries more detail than one person can lay out consistently: a Deming Prize roadmap, for instance, spans dozens of activities across several years.

This was a throughput constraint rather than a quality one. TQMI's consultants are experienced and its methods are rigorous. Senior judgment is the scarce input, and a significant share of it was going into assembling documents instead of the thinking that makes them worth reading.

How the rollout was run

Caizin did not ask TQMI's consultants to change how they work. Claude was placed on the surfaces they already used, and each consultant applied it to the deliverable in front of them.

The chat window took on research and pressure-testing ahead of a client visit or a draft. The workspace turned source material, such as a training transcript or a set of sales records, into a finished artifact. Claude inside MS Office produced the Word documents, decks, and spreadsheets that clients actually receive.

Two working rules kept the output fit to send. The first was matching the surface and the model to the job. On the post-programme tutor listed below, a lighter model returned wrong answers while a stronger model at a higher effort setting returned correct ones, and the team standardised on the setting that proved reliable. The second rule was consultant review of every client-facing output. In all ten use cases a consultant checked the work, corrected it, and decided what the client saw.

The work

Ten use cases, from four consultants.

Three are set out in full below, chosen because the consultants who ran them measured the effect. The remaining seven follow in summary. Individual consultants are not named, and client identities are kept to a broad sector at most.

Case one  ·  Sales analytics
A sales KPI dashboard that recommends action

A field sales team tracks around 17 KPIs per officer as part of its daily routine. A branch head with ten officers therefore faces roughly 170 numbers in a single morning stand-up, which is more than anyone can review properly. A TQMI consultant used Claude to build a dashboard that identifies each officer's specific gaps and proposes an action against each KPI.

The stand-up became more focused, officers could see their own performance next to the branch's, and the client received recommendations it had not had before. The consultant put the data and the recommendations through several validation passes before taking anything to the client.

Case two  ·  Client process manual
A beat planning manual delivered in three days

Beat planning sets which market a sales officer covers on which day, and one client needed a full manual for it. Written by hand, including the market research the manual requires, the consultant estimated several weeks to a month of work.

Around 70 percent of the manual was drafted using Claude in the workspace and in MS Office. The consultant's own time went into proofreading and editing it to fit the client's business. The capacity that came free went into starting other process work earlier.

Case three  ·  Deming Prize programme
Deliverables defined for a four-year quality roadmap

A heavy-industry manufacturer pursuing the Deming Prize needed a defined deliverable for every activity in its improvement roadmap, covering more than 28 activities across four years. The established method was repeated working sessions and step-by-step drafting.

The consultant wrote a single detailed prompt carrying the full programme context and generated deliverables for every activity at once. He then reviewed each one for fit and risk, removing or rewording anything that read as too constraining or too superficial before it reached the client.

The other seven use cases
Use caseWhat was doneEffect
Workforce attrition analysis Raw plant workforce data turned into a client-ready presentation: 66 separations broken down by tenure, department, floor, role, and supervisor, all of them established as early exits without formal notice. A spreadsheet exercise became an executive readout with a prioritised roadmap for onboarding, mentoring, and root-cause follow-up.
Exception detection on daily operations data Claude reads the daily parameter report from an operation with hundreds of process units, each logging dozens of readings, and flags the units outside normal ranges. The team works the exceptions rather than scanning the full report by hand, which was slow and easy to get wrong.
Competitor benchmarking study The strengths of two competitors compiled and converted into a prioritised recommendation set, each item carrying an effort-versus-impact rating that the consultant reviewed and adjusted. Recommendations were easier for the client to prioritise, and the structure reduced rework.
Quality-circle project on assembly-line rejection An 8-step quality-circle presentation assembled in TQMI's PDCA format, with fishbone and why-why analysis, a countermeasure matrix, before-and-after results, and a standardisation plan. Methodology and numbers came from the project. Claude handled the structuring and layout that usually takes hours per deck.
QC Story on welding defects A 10-step QC Story presentation built to the same methodology, from theme selection and current-state data through countermeasures, results, and standardisation. A second deck produced to the firm's standard format without the usual assembly time.
Post-programme training tutor The transcript of a delivered training programme turned into a tutor instructed to answer only from that transcript, so participants who missed a session could ask about the material. A working test rather than a product. Reaching participants would need a front end and a hosted transcript, which TQMI treats as a defined next step.
Pre-visit assessment research Ahead of a first-time assessment for an automotive client, research into the typical issues in that kind of operation, common customer concerns, and points of comparison with competitors, alongside the usual review of weak KPIs, past experience, and client feedback. Confirmed the hypotheses the consultant had formed before the site visit and surfaced areas that might have been missed. Findings were validated with direct questions on site.
Results

What the consultants reported.

The figures below are as reported by the consultants who ran each use case, and the pattern across the ten is consistent.

Speed
25–75%
Time removed on the tasks where consultants measured it, with the document-heavy work at the top of that range. The beat planning manual is the clearest case, moving from an estimated month to three days.
Quality
Closer to client-ready
Consultants reported greater rigour and more consistency, with drafts that needed tailoring rather than rewriting. One said the sales work "gave the speed and clarity" to reach a point that a conventional change-management programme would have taken months and material cost to reach.
Control
Consultant sign-off
Every client-facing output was reviewed by the consultant who owned it. Several validation passes preceded the KPI dashboard, and every roadmap deliverable was checked for fit and risk before the client saw it.

Work previously measured in weeks was completed in days. A second consultant described the pre-visit research as giving "focused, technical depth and relevant value to customer". Model choice mattered on the training tutor, where the consultant settled on the configuration that returned correct answers. Drafting and assembly moved to the tool, while the decision about what was right for the client stayed with the consultant.

What made the difference

Three factors behind the results.

The tools sat inside the existing workflow. The same assistant was useful in a chat window for research, in a workspace for building an artifact from source material, and inside MS Office for the final document, so consultants got the benefit without stepping outside the tools they already used.

The drafts arrived close to client grade. Across the ten use cases the work left over was review and tailoring rather than rewriting from scratch, and that is where the time savings came from.

Configuration was treated as part of the method. On the training tutor, matching the model and the effort level to the task separated wrong answers from reliable ones, which gave TQMI a repeatable way to get dependable output.

Across ten use cases, 25 to 75 percent of the task time came out where it was measured, and a consultant reviewed every output before a client saw it.

What this points to

An early sample rather than
the finished picture.

For TQMI, the ten use cases came from four consultants applying Claude to their own deliverables. The same method, putting the tool on real client work and keeping consultant review in place, applies across the rest of the practice: quality-circle programmes, attrition analysis, executive readouts, and proposals.

For Caizin, the engagement is a working example of AI adoption outside a demonstration. The consultant keeps the client relationship and the judgment calls. The research and assembly that used to cap how much any one consultant could produce move to the tool.
